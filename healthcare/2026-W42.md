## The week in one paragraph

The week's regulatory change is about data, not AI. On **5 October**, HHS (through CMS), Labor and the Treasury issued the **Transparency in Coverage final rules (CMS-9882-F)**. These rules rework the price files that health plans have published since July 2022. The files have been huge and messy, and few people could use them. The new rules cut provider-rate pairs that would likely never be paid. They file rates **per provider network** instead of per plan. They set **one format (the Departments intend JSON)**, add **taxonomy and utilization files**, lower the allowed-amount threshold from **20 to 11 claims**, and move updates from **monthly to quarterly**. A named senior official must also attest that the files are accurate. For anyone who analyzes negotiated rates, the dataset is about to get smaller, cleaner and less frequent. FDA's newsroom had no AI-device action this week. The **19 October** comment deadline on the generative-AI device discussion paper is still open.

## Items

- **[Transparency in Coverage Final Rules (CMS-9882-F)](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/transparency-coverage-final-rules-cms-9882-f)** `topic:payment`. **Agencies:** HHS (CMS), Labor, Treasury. **Stage:** final rules, fact sheet dated **5 October 2026**, with a [CMS press release](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/new-regulations-make-it-easier-find-compare-report-healthcare-pricing-coverage-information) the same day. **Binds:** non-grandfathered group health plans and issuers offering non-grandfathered group or individual coverage. **What changes:**
  - *In-network rate files* drop provider-rate combinations that providers are unlikely to be paid for, based on provider taxonomy or claims rules, and move from one file per plan to **one file per provider network**.
  - *New files:* a **Taxonomy File**, a **Utilization File** for each in-network rate file, and a `.txt` file that points to the file locations and lists a monitored contact email.
  - *Allowed-amount files* are aggregated by market type (large group, small group, individual, self-insured), and the reporting threshold falls from **20 to 11 claims**.
  - Files add product type (e.g. HMO, PPO) and network name and ID. Updates move **from monthly to quarterly**. Plans must use a single format, which the Departments intend to be **JSON**.
  - A **CEO, president or designated senior official** must be named in an accuracy attestation. Homepages need a "Price Transparency" or "Transparency in Coverage" footer link. Cost-sharing data from the self-service tool must also be available **by phone** on request.

  **Dates:** effective **60 days after Federal Register publication**. In-network and allowed-amount changes apply **five months** after publication, and taxonomy, utilization and text-file changes apply **11 months** after. Self-service tool changes apply to plan years beginning on or after **1 January 2027**. **Estimated net savings:** about **$174.5 million a year** from the second year. *CMS fact sheet fetched 2026-10-12. It does not give the publication date: the public-inspection document is 2026-20447, which this run could not open, so the calendar applicability dates are not pinned down here.*

## So what

*For healthcare data scientists, ML engineers in healthcare, and healthcare executives.*

- **Healthcare data scientists:** The **Transparency in Coverage** price files are about to change shape. Rates will come per network, not per plan, in one format (likely JSON). Junk rates that never get paid will be cut. That should make the files smaller and easier to use. But updates drop from monthly to quarterly, and allowed amounts get grouped by market type. If you track prices month to month, you will lose detail. Write down now which of your analyses depend on monthly data or plan-level files.
- **ML engineers in healthcare:** Any pipeline that reads these files will break when the new layout arrives. That could be about five months after the rule is printed in the Federal Register. Plan for new files too: a taxonomy file (provider types) and a utilization file (how often rates are used). Build your parser so it can read both the old and new formats for a while. Nothing here touches patient data. These are price files.
- **Healthcare executives:** If you run a health plan, a named top official must now sign off that the files are right. That puts real people on the hook for data quality. The government expects plans to save about **$174.5 million a year** from year two. If you are a hospital or buyer, cleaner rate data makes it easier for rivals and employers to compare your prices. Ask your pricing team how you look against others now, before the clean data comes out.

### Problems worth taking on

**Check your price data before the new files land.** *Problem:* Plans and hospitals pay vendors to clean up rate files that are huge and full of junk rows. *Why now:* The **CMS-9882-F** rules cut junk rates and require one network-level format and a signed accuracy attestation. *First step:* Take one network's current in-network file. Have a data scientist and the contracting lead mark which rates were really paid last year. Success is a clear count of how much of the file the new rule would remove.
